# Candidate 007 · Stage X — Authority, Rights, Risk, and Governance

Editorial status: DRAFT — citations pending, not publication-approved.

185. The capability contract developed in the previous stage raises a harder question: who is permitted to put that capability into motion? A workflow can be technically able to send a customer file, issue an invoice, or publish a lesson without being authorized to do any of those things. Availability of a command is therefore not evidence of permission. Before designing an automated step, name the human or institution with legitimate authority, the specific action being delegated, and the conditions under which that delegation ends. The learner should be able to explain why a successful technical call might still be an unacceptable business action.

186. Authority becomes clearer when a workflow is described in roles rather than personalities. The customer requests an outcome; the operator accepts or rejects the request; a service performs permitted steps; a reviewer checks evidence; and a designated approver authorizes release. One person may occupy several roles in a small business, but their responsibilities should still be explicit. Imagine a copy-and-ship order involving sensitive legal documents. The employee who prepares the file need not possess authority to broaden its distribution. Map each role to its permitted decisions and identify the conflict that appears when the same account can both change and approve an artifact.

187. Least privilege turns role definitions into operational boundaries. A research assistant gathering public material should not automatically inherit the credentials needed to modify a production manuscript. A graphics worker exporting an approved slide needs access to the specified asset, not to every customer file in the repository. Permissions should be assigned by task, resource, and duration wherever the platform permits. Review access when the work changes, not only when an employee joins or leaves. The guided question is practical: if a task is compromised or mistaken, what is the maximum damage the assigned permissions would allow?

188. A generic approval message can conceal an important change. Suppose an operator approves one lesson package but the underlying text, destination, or licensing terms are modified before execution. The earlier consent should not silently apply to the new object. A stronger process binds approval to the exact proposed action and a reproducible digest of the material being released. The system must also check that the approved digest still matches at execution time. Learners should distinguish approving a category of activity from approving a specific artifact, recipient, and effect. Which elements must be fixed before the human approval is meaningful?

189. Even a precisely bound approval can fail if it is accepted indefinitely. A release token captured from one successful operation should not authorize the same effect again, nor should yesterday's permission silently extend into a new job. Replay resistance can include a unique request identifier, expiration, consumption record, and a check that the approval belongs to the correct actor and operation. These controls matter when retries are common: a network timeout may leave the worker uncertain whether payment or publication already occurred. The learner should design the desired outcome first, then determine how the system prevents a second unintended execution.

190. A policy that can be bypassed is advice, not an enforceable control. Imagine a team requiring every protected write to pass through a gateway, while a worker still has credentials and network access to call the destination directly. A test of the normal route may pass even though the unsafe route remains open. The relevant question is not whether the gateway evaluates policy correctly when asked; it is whether every consequential route must ask. Learners should sketch all possible paths from task initiation to external effect, including background jobs, direct APIs, browser sessions, and emergency tools. Any untested path is an unresolved control claim.

191. An authorization record must identify more than the name of a tool. It should distinguish the accountable human actor, the service identity executing the call, the tenant or customer context, and the exact resources involved. Shared credentials obscure this chain because different actions appear to come from one undifferentiated principal. In a multi-agent workflow, a label such as 'research dot' is not sufficient unless identity is authenticated and bound to permissions. The operator should ask whether the audit record can establish who requested an action, which service carried it out, and which authorization justified it. If those answers are missing, attribution is incomplete.

192. Evidence should be retained before an irreversible effect, not assembled afterward from convenient fragments. For a manuscript release, this may mean preserving the approved source revision, destination, reviewer decision, permitted transformation, and anticipated artifact identifiers before publishing begins. A post hoc screenshot cannot reliably prove which version was actually authorized. Durable custody also provides a recovery point when a service fails between preparation and delivery. Learners should model the moment just before effect: which facts must already be written to a persistent record, and which facts can only be recorded after the result returns?

193. A shared infrastructure business may serve several customers through one workflow engine. That efficiency creates a strict separation requirement: one customer's documents, credentials, prompts, approvals, and evidence must not become available to another. Adding a tenant label to a report is not the same as enforcing boundaries in storage, execution, and retrieval. Consider two training providers using a common publishing worker; neither should be able to inspect the other's unpublished assessment bank. The learner should identify where tenant identity is checked, how cross-tenant requests are rejected, and how negative tests would demonstrate that the isolation actually holds.

194. Browser automation introduces a different source of authority confusion. The page being read may contain instructions, but those instructions originate with an outside party rather than with the operator. A malicious website can ask an assistant to reveal data, visit another address, or modify a file while appearing to provide ordinary search results. The workflow should treat page contents as untrusted task data and restrict the actions a browsing worker can perform. For a publishing research task, the browser may retrieve source information without inheriting permission to upload manuscript files. Which observable tests would show that this separation survives hostile page content?

195. Prompt injection is not the same as an authorized operator instruction, even when it is written in the imperative voice. An external PDF, scraped article, or retrieved comment may demand that the system ignore its approval rules. The document has evidentiary relevance only for the research question; it cannot promote itself into governance authority. The useful design exercise is to trace trust boundaries between user instructions, tool results, source documents, and executable actions. A responsible team documents known failure modes and tests refusal paths rather than assuming that a reassuring instruction in the prompt is a sufficient defense.

196. Data minimization is also a production design choice. An illustrator assigned to produce generic artwork may need a paragraph's theme but not the customer's original invoices, private correspondence, or full manuscript archive. A citation checker may need a claim and source URL without unrestricted access to financial accounts. Reducing data exposure protects privacy and can also simplify review. The learner should examine one workflow field at a time and ask what is necessary for the stated purpose, how long it must be retained, and who can access it. More context is not automatically safer or more useful.

197. Content rights must be settled before a generated lesson is treated as a sellable asset. A publicly accessible image is not automatically licensed for commercial reproduction, and permission to analyze a dataset is not necessarily permission to distribute its tables. The same distinction applies to course excerpts, vendor logos, assessment items, and customer-provided documents. In an educational publishing operation, rights are not an administrative afterthought; they determine which production route is allowed. Learners should create a rights register that names each source, its owner or licensor, the permitted uses, the evidence of permission, and any required attribution or restrictions.

198. A rights register only helps when permissions can be connected to actual outputs. Suppose one illustration can appear in an instructor guide but not in a commercially sold workbook. The production system should prevent that asset from silently moving into the wrong edition. Asset identifiers, license terms, expiration dates, and output destinations need a traceable relationship. When a license is uncertain, the correct state is not 'probably acceptable'; it is unresolved and held for review. The guided exercise asks learners to trace one item from acquisition through editing, slide inclusion, print export, and future revision. At which step could an unnoticed rights violation occur?

199. Privacy obligations depend on what data the workflow actually handles and where it travels. A course creator collecting only public research references faces a different risk from a training provider processing student records, identity documents, or regulated professional data. Vendor contracts, storage regions, access policies, and retention rules therefore need to follow the use case rather than a generic promise of compliance. Learners should identify which data categories enter a proposed service and which legal or contractual requirements require expert review. A privacy statement drafted by a model does not establish compliance with those obligations.

200. Risk assessment becomes useful when it changes design decisions. Listing a risk as 'high' without explaining why does not tell the operator what control to build or test. For each important failure, describe its cause, affected parties, plausible impact, existing safeguards, and remaining uncertainty. Then decide whether to reduce the risk, restrict the workflow, transfer part of the responsibility, or decline the action. A regulated course publication and a routine internal training memo may warrant different gates even if both are produced by the same tools. The learner should connect each risk rating to a specific operational consequence.

201. Human authority should not be reduced to an exhausted reviewer clicking approve on an endless queue. If every low-risk spelling correction requires the same attention as a publication with legal consequences, the approval system may become a ceremonial bottleneck. Effective governance assigns decision thresholds: some changes can be executed within a preapproved envelope, while material changes demand explicit review. Those thresholds must be established by the accountable operator rather than improvised by the worker. Learners should decide which approvals protect genuine consequences and which can be delegated through documented rules without sacrificing accountability.

202. Exceptions reveal whether a service can function responsibly outside ideal conditions. A corrupted source file, ambiguous customer instruction, missing citation, or failed payment confirmation should move into a defined hold or escalation path. The system should not guess what the operator would have wanted simply to keep throughput high. For each exception, the record should show the blocking condition, responsible reviewer, available evidence, safe next actions, and deadline where applicable. The guided exercise gives learners an apparently routine job with one missing permission. Can they specify a recovery procedure that does not bypass the missing authorization?

203. Logs are valuable only when they can answer real questions. A system that records thousands of debug lines may still fail to show the manuscript version used for a slide, the source behind a disputed statistic, or the person who approved release. Design the audit record around consequential events: request received, identity verified, inputs selected, policy evaluated, artifact generated, review completed, effect attempted, and result confirmed or failed. Sensitive material should not be copied into logs without necessity. Learners should identify the minimum event fields needed to reconstruct a disputed transaction while protecting information that does not belong in the record.

204. A governance rule is not proven merely because its expected behavior is written in a policy document. It needs tests that attempt to defeat it. A healthy test suite includes allowed paths, denied paths, stale approvals, altered artifact digests, mismatched identities, direct-transport bypasses, cross-tenant access, and hostile content retrieved during browsing. Some tests should intentionally fail to demonstrate that the controls stop an unsafe action before an external effect occurs. Learners should distinguish a synthetic policy evaluation from evidence that the production network makes bypass impossible. Which test would reveal the gap between those two claims?

205. Not every residual risk can be eliminated, and a review decision should say what remains unknown. An operator may authorize a narrow pilot while blocking payments, external publication, or customer data movement. That decision is more informative than an unqualified 'approved' label because it states the envelope within which the evidence is sufficient. The record should include the tested controls, exceptions, unresolved risks, permitted scope, reviewer, and expiration or reapproval trigger. The learner's task is to write a decision that a future operator could enforce without needing to infer its boundaries from an old conversation.

206. This stage closes with a release gate rather than a declaration of confidence. Before a capability acts on behalf of a customer or publisher, verify that authority is authentic, approval matches the exact effect, necessary evidence is in custody, tenant boundaries hold, and untrusted sources cannot rewrite instructions. An incomplete test does not mean the workflow is worthless; it means the allowed deployment must remain correspondingly narrow. The next stage asks how multiple specialized services can cooperate within these constraints. HAVE — Human Authority, Verified Execution — connects permission, observable behavior, and the human decision to proceed.
